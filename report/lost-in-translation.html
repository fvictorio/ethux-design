The role of language
in web3 adoption

We tracked six months of wallet clicks across the 36 language editions of ethereum.org's wallet list. Then we read the translation files of major apps such as Uniswap, Aave and 1inch. The short version: in Brazil, the six wallets available in Portuguese collect nine of every ten clicks, the pattern repeats across Asia, and most apps have not entered the race.

The market

Where a translation
wins users.

ethereum.org, the Ethereum ecosystem's main education site, keeps a list of 49 wallets and publishes it in 36 languages. On each language edition, wallets available in that language appear at the top; the rest of the order is random. That design makes reader preference measurable. If people clicked whatever sits on top, the top wallets would collect a predictable share of clicks. Everything beyond that share means readers went looking for their language.

The result splits the world in two. In Brazil, Taiwan, Thailand, Indonesia, Vietnam, Russia and China, readers chose wallets in their language far beyond what top placement explains. In Poland, Czechia, Ukraine and the Arabic edition, readers scrolled past those wallets and picked global brands. A translation buys users in the first group and buys little in the second.

The catch

A translation only pays
if it's right.

There is one trap in this budget line. Machine translation produces text that looks right, and nobody on the team can tell when it is wrong. Aave, one of the largest lending apps, publishes a Russian translation in which four core terms carry the wrong meaning. Its Spanish version has none of these errors. The difference is a native speaker reading the file before users do.

Aave · Russian · four terms that move money
Repay→Вознаграждать→means "reward"
Collateral→Побочный→means "side / secondary"
Stake→Столб→means "pillar / post"
Withdraw→Отводить→means "divert away"
Machine translation produced plausible Russian, so nothing looked broken. Budget for a native speaker to read the terms that move money. Leaving jargon in English has a cost too: "slippage" untranslated hides the one number protecting a trader from a bad price.

Quality is one half of the picture; existence is the other. We took the ten most used consumer apps on Ethereum and its layer-2 networks, ranked by active addresses, and checked which languages their interfaces actually ship. Four of the ten are English only.

The fix

A shared glossary,
and a reason to use it.

Two things would change this picture. The first is a shared glossary: an open list of the fifty or so crypto terms that move money (send, repay, collateral, withdraw, seed phrase) with one agreed translation per language, so every team ships the same correct words instead of inventing its own.

The second is a reason to use it. Wallets tell ethereum.org which languages they support, and the site ranks them higher on those language editions. Today it takes the wallet's word for it; nobody checks the translation exists or is any good. If that ranking counted only translations reviewed by a native speaker, getting fifty terms right would directly win a wallet placement in front of readers who are minutes from choosing one.

AI drafts everything

Every term in every language can be machine-generated in a day. Drafting was never the hard part.

02

Native speakers decide the hard calls

Keep "slippage" in English or translate it? Coin a word that does not exist yet? The people who read the language decide, and sign the decision.

03

Apps and ethereum.org adopt it

Teams pull the glossary instead of translating from scratch, and ethereum.org rewards wallets whose translations passed a native review with better placement.

An honest example of why humans stay in the loop. The current draft glossary translates "slippage" to deslizamiento with high confidence, yet the live apps keep the English "Slippage". Which is right is a native-speaker judgment. That call is the work.

If you hold a budget

What to do with this.

Wallet teams

Pick a market from the top group of the chart. Indonesian, Vietnamese, Thai and Brazilian Portuguese have measured demand and single-digit competition. Fifty core terms plus a native review is a small sprint, and it competes for thousands of monthly high-intent clicks.

App teams

Four of the ten most used apps ship no translations at all; the graded table shows who. If you do translate, have a native speaker read the terms that move money before your users do. We share flagged terms on request and will gladly re-audit.

Translators

Roughly fifty safety-critical terms in your language need a ruling: translate, transliterate, or keep the English. The contested calls in the glossary are open for exactly this.

ethereum.org

One criterion added to the wallet listing policy, the language boost applies to verified translations only, makes quality worth money to every listed wallet. The audit script and the termbase are ready to serve it.
