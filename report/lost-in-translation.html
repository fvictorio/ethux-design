The role of language
in web3 adoption.

We tracked six months of wallet clicks across the 36 language editions of ethereum.org's wallet list, and read the translation files major apps actually ship. In much of the world, language decides which wallet gets the click. Very few teams are competing for it.

The market

Where a translation
wins users.

ethereum.org lists 49 wallets and puts the ones available in the reader's language at the top. The rest of the order is random, which makes this measurable: we can separate clicks a wallet earns by sitting high on the list from clicks readers give it because it speaks their language. Six months, 36 language editions, about 40,000 outbound clicks.

Each bar: the share of that edition's outbound clicks going to wallets available in the reader's language. The line: the share those same wallets would get from their list position alone. A bar past its line means readers went looking for their language.

The chart splits the world in two. In Brazil, Taiwan, Thailand, Indonesia, Vietnam, Russia and China, readers went well past what placement explains: they wanted a wallet in their language and clicked accordingly. In Poland, Czechia, Ukraine and the Arabic edition, readers scrolled past the local-language wallets and picked global brands, so a translation buys little there.

For anyone allocating a localization budget, that is the priority list. And the traffic is worth winning: these are people mid-decision. On the Chinese edition, 87 of every 100 visits end with a click out to a wallet website. On the English page it is 36.

The catch

A translation only pays
if it's right.

Before that budget goes to a vendor, one warning from the other half of this research. We read the translation files six dapps and Binance actually ship, in Portuguese, Spanish and Russian. Most of what differs between apps is harmless synonym choice. A few things reverse meaning. Aave's Russian interface labels its repay button with the Russian word for reward, and mistranslates collateral, stake and withdraw the same way: a plausible word carrying the wrong sense. The same app's Spanish gets all four right. The Russian file shipped without a native review, in an app that manages loans.

Aave · Russian · four terms that move money
Repay→Вознаграждать→means "reward"
Collateral→Побочный→means "side / secondary"
Stake→Столб→means "pillar / post"
Withdraw→Отводить→means "divert away"
Machine translation produced plausible Russian, so nothing looked broken, and it shipped. Budget for a native speaker to read the terms that move money. Leaving jargon in English has a cost too: "slippage" untranslated hides the one number protecting a trader from a bad price.

The other cost of translating alone is vocabulary. Your users arrive having already learned terms in other apps, and today those apps disagree, app to app and sometimes screen to screen. Here is how six actions read across the apps we audited:

The play

Fifty terms, done right,
earn distribution.

The language flags wallets declare on ethereum.org already carry real weight: on the Russian edition, nine of every ten clicks go to wallets flagged as supporting Russian. Today that flag is taken on trust, and Aave shows what an unreviewed translation can hide. We are proposing the ranking boost move to verified translations. For a wallet team the mechanics stay simple: translate the roughly fifty terms that move money (send, repay, collateral, withdraw, seed phrase), have a native speaker sign off, and the placement follows. There is a shared glossary so nobody has to invent the vocabulary alone.

01

AI drafts everything

Every term, every language, generated and published instantly. Translation was never the bottleneck.

02

Native speakers rule the hard calls

Contested choices go to the people who read them: translate "slippage" or keep it? Coin a word that does not exist yet? They decide and put their name on it.

03

Verification earns the boost

Apps pull the termbase; auditors and LLMs cite it via an open API; listing surfaces reward the wallets that pass. One steward decision fixes the word everywhere at once.

An honest example of why humans stay in the loop. The current draft glossary translates "slippage" to deslizamiento with high confidence, yet the live apps keep the English "Slippage". Which is right is a native-speaker judgment. That call is the work.

If you hold a budget

What to do with this.

Wallet teams

Pick a market from the top group of the chart. Indonesian, Vietnamese, Thai and Brazilian Portuguese have measured demand and single-digit competition. Fifty core terms plus a native review is a small sprint, and it competes for thousands of monthly high-intent clicks.

Dapp teams

Your locale files are public. Have a native speaker read the terms that move money before your users do. We share flagged terms on request and will gladly re-audit.

Translators

Roughly fifty safety-critical terms in your language need a ruling: translate, transliterate, or keep the English. The contested calls in the glossary are open for exactly this.

ethereum.org

One criterion added to the wallet listing policy, the language boost applies to verified translations only, makes quality worth money to every listed wallet. The audit script and the termbase are ready to serve it.
